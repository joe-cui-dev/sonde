The keeper's logbook had not been opened in eleven years, and the first thing Ines noticed was that the last entry stopped mid-sentence.

"Wind NNE, sea moderate, visibility good. At 0340 the lamp" — and then nothing, only the ruled lines running on down the page, each one empty.

She had come out on the supply boat that morning with two suitcases and a letter from the trust that owned the island, confirming her six-month contract to catalogue whatever was left in the tower before it was sold. The boatman had carried her bags up the jetty and then stood there a while, looking at the lighthouse as if it owed him money.

"You'll want to keep the radio charged," he said. "Weather turns quick out here."

Now it was late afternoon, and the light through the lantern-room windows had gone the color of weak tea. Ines sat on the iron stairs with the logbook open across her knees. The handwriting was small and upright, the kind taught in schools that no longer existed. Every entry before the last one was complete: wind, sea, visibility, the hour the lamp was lit, the hour it was put out.

Below her, somewhere in the base of the tower, a door she was sure she had closed banged once against its frame.
